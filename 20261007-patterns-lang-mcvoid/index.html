THE ONE PAGE

contextSomeone has to understand a system they did not build, in one sitting.

problemThe truth is spread across 473 files, 170 routes and three roots. Any page that shows all of it shows none of it.

forcesCompleteness pulls toward the index. Comprehension pulls toward one page. They do not meet in the middle — a half-index is the worst of both.

solutionCompose ONE page that answers the one question a human actually has. Everything else is reachable from it and is not rendered until asked for. The page is a reduction, never a digest.

THE TITLE

contextA page needs to say what it is before it says anything else.

problemA page that opens with chrome makes the reader work out where they are.

forcesBranding wants a logo. Orientation wants words. Words win — a name is readable by a machine and a person at once.

solutionOne big monospace title. No wordmark, no hero image. The title is the address of the object on the page.

THE META STRIP

contextThe reader needs to trust the page before they read it.

problemProvenance lives in footnotes nobody reaches. The reader cannot tell a live page from a dead one.

forcesBurying provenance keeps the header clean and makes every claim unverifiable. Putting all of it up top makes the page a status report.

solutionONE strip of facts directly under the title: what it is, when it moved, who it is for, how stale it is. State the AGE, never just the timestamp — a timestamp that reads "now" while being history is a lie by omission.

THE PIVOT

contextA page has one thing it wants you to understand.

problemDense pages open with context and bury the point on line 40.

forcesContext-first is how papers work. Point-first is how a person decides whether to keep reading. A page is not a paper.

solutionA lede that states the single claim, and a way to mark the exact pivot word. Then everything below is evidence for that sentence.

THE CARD

contextA claim needs to be checkable without leaving the page.

problemA bare claim in prose has no boundary — the reader cannot tell where the claim stops and the commentary starts.

forcesBoxes read as decoration unless they carry a verdict. A card that only frames is a rounded rectangle, and the house rules forbid that grammar.

solutionA self-contained claim with an inverted badge carrying the VERDICT (verified / candidate / failed). Hard edges. The badge is the only colour in the block.

THE DO BLOCK

contextThe reader has understood and now has to act.

problemMost pages end with more prose. The reader has to re-derive the instruction from the argument they just read.

forcesRepeating the argument is generous and useless. A bare command is terse and rude. The action needs the same dignity as the analysis.

solutionOne block, phrased as the move: what to do, in the imperative, with the smallest possible scope. If there are many moves, they are ranked, not listed — see 07.

THE STALE FLAG

contextA projection prints a timestamp.

problemA projection that prints a timestamp without its age is a state line that reads as "now" while being history. Measured: a route could read "09-30 18:50" while being days old, and nothing said so.

forcesAlways showing age adds noise to every row. Never showing it makes the whole surface untrustworthy. The flag must never be out-specified by a surrounding rule.

solutionState the age beside the stamp, and past the clock's interval (2x the refresh cadence) mark it in the host's attention token. THE FLAG MUST BE UN-LOSABLE: one rule, `!important`, because 65 context rules will otherwise swallow it silently.

  • / (52 flags live, measured 2026-10-06)

THE MOVES

contextThe reader wants to know what to do next, not what is true in general.

problemA page that is entirely analysis hands the decision back to the reader.

forcesRanked actions imply authority the writer may not have. A flat list implies they are equivalent when they are not.

solutionA ranked block of actions placed BEFORE the detail. Rank is a claim and must be defensible: the first move is the one that unblocks the others.

THE TABLE

contextTwo or more things must be compared, or a correction must be shown.

problemComparison in prose forces the reader to hold both sides in their head. A correction buried in prose is read as an aside.

forcesTables are dense and hostile on a phone. Prose is warm and imprecise. The house grammar prefers borders and typography over containers.

solutionA hard-edged table for comparison, and for corrections: what it said / what is true. Corrections are first-class content, not footnotes — a wrong number that stays wrong is worse than no number.

THE BADGE

contextA card needs a verdict at a glance.

problemColour coding states invites a palette; every new state adds a colour and the page turns into a dashboard.

forcesSemantic colour is legible and grows without bound. Two tokens are stable and stop meaning anything.

solutionOne INVERTED chip — solid background, knocked-out text. It carries a state word. The host's two tokens (`--hot`, `--ok`) are the only colours allowed to mean something; everything else is ink and muted ink.

THE CLOSE

contextThe reader is finished and about to leave.

problemPages trail off. The reader is never told the argument concluded.

forcesA summary repeats. A call to action sells. Neither is what a page owes the reader at the end.

solutionONE box before the foot. It restates the pivot in one sentence and stops. No summary, no "thanks for reading".

THE PROVENANCE FOOT

contextEvery claim on the page came from somewhere.

problemSources live in files the reader cannot see. The page asserts without showing work.

forcesFull provenance is unreadable. No provenance is unfalsifiable.

solutionThe foot names the artifacts, commits, PRs and measurements the page stands on, in the form a machine can check: SHA, path, date. If you cannot name it, the claim does not go on the page.

THE FILTERS

contextA page carries more rows than a person will read.

problemA long list is honest and unusable. A short list is usable and dishonest.

forcesPagination hides. Infinite scroll removes the sense of scale. Search alone presumes the reader knows the vocabulary.

solutionA sticky search field plus LANE CHIPS that name the states the rows can be in. The chips are derived from the data, not invented — if a lane has no rows it does not get a chip. Always show the total against the filtered count so the reader knows what they are not seeing.

THE UN-LOSABLE RULE

contextOne visual property must never be wrong.

problemCSS is a specificity contest. Every later rule that sets the same property wins silently. Measured 2026-10-06: a flag applied to 52 elements, the weight applied, the COLOUR never landed — 65 context rules could swallow it.

forces`!important` is a code smell. A silently-broken invariant is a worse one. Patching each context is the actual bug, because the next context rule wins again.

solutionDeclare the invariant ONCE, un-losable (`!important`), and write a CHECKER that asserts it landed — not that the class was applied. Class-present is not colour-landed.

THE CHECKER

contextA rule has been written down.

problemA rule nobody runs is a wish. Measured across this system: specified-but-never-scheduled is the single most expensive kind of debt — a script designed but not scheduled costs the same as one never written.

forcesGates cost runs and slow everyone down. No gates means every rule is decorative.

solutionEvery law gets a pass/fail checker with a real exit code, and it is run where the change happens. A checker that asserts an artifact EXISTS and never asserts it WORKS is the commonest failure — see 13.

THE HOLDING PEN

contextSomeone ships a throwaway page with a date in its name.

problemDated pages accumulate forever. The index fills with dead objects and the field starts to look like a landfill.

forcesDeleting early destroys work still in use. Keeping forever destroys the index. A naming convention alone does not expire anything.

solutionA REGISTRY of temporary objects with an explicit expiry, an index page showing the countdowns, and a check that exits non-zero when a dated object is NOT registered — so the un-registered case is loud instead of invisible. Expired objects move to a holding pen, never a hard delete.

AREA IS EFFORT

contextYou want a map of where the work actually went.

problemTreemaps are usually sized by file size or member count. That measures the shape of the tree, not the labour spent on it.

forcesCommit count is easy and lies — machine refreshes inflate it. File size is easy and lies — generated artifacts are huge. Effort has no direct metric.

solutionSize the rectangle by the count of SEMANTIC touches, with machine-refresh commits stripped. Hue is state, brightness is freshness, area is work landed. Say the encoding on the page; an unexplained colour is decoration.

THE PULSE

contextYou want to see how something is moving over time, not just its state now.

problemA timeline of events is unreadable at 180 routes. A sparkline per row is 180 sparklines.

forcesAggregate charts hide the outliers. Per-row charts do not fit.

solutionONE lane per state, with each object plotted at its last semantic touch. You see clusters and gaps as shape. The absence of points IS the information.

REDUCE, DON'T EXPAND

contextA human needs to read a knowledge base built for machines.

problemThe natural response to "I can't find anything" is a better index. That is more surface, and the problem was never finding — it was being handed the wrong kind of page.

forcesA digest is generous and enormous. A reduction is small and omits. Omitting feels like losing information.

solutionWrite the page a human would read, capped in size, and label the expansion as the expansion. Most of a machine knowledge base is WRITTEN FOR SEARCH, not for reading — that is usually the system working. The failure is handing a human one of those.

  • wiki/personal/ (3 pages, capped at 30)

COMMIT ON CHANGE

contextA generator runs on a timer and commits the output.

problemA refresh commit that only moves a clock makes the chronology unlistenable and trains everyone to ignore the log. Measured: 46 changed lines in a pure refresh, 25 of them pure clock, 21 real state.

forcesA real-time page wants to always be current. The git record wants to mean something. They are the same file.

solutionCompare the generated output with its timestamps STRIPPED. If the non-clock content is identical, do not commit. Real state changes still land; the clock does not.

RECOVER BEFORE INVENTING

contextYou are about to build something.

problemThe new thing is satisfying and the existing thing is unglamorous. Building beside the existing thing is the default and it is how a field turns into a landfill.

forcesExisting artifacts are half-right, which makes them feel unusable. Reusing them means inheriting their compromises.

solutionFind the host first. Is this a DELTA to an existing head, a DONOR to one, or genuinely a missing function? Zero obvious host is an UNRESOLVED submission, not a new project. Build on the artifact even when the artifact is imperfect — see 21.

THE PARTIAL ASSET

contextThe obvious host is 80% right and 20% wrong.

problemDiscarding it and starting clean is cleaner and loses the 80%. Keeping it means working around the 20%.

forcesPurity of the new design. Continuity with what already shipped and is already known.

solutionTake the 80% and fix the 20% IN PLACE. Measured this week: `house-patterns.css` was already numbered like a pattern language and nobody had written the language down — the fix was to transcribe it, not to author a new palette.
