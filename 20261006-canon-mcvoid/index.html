Where the doing goes. Into the metal. Into the noun. Into the person who made it. Into the crowd. Into the weights. Into the company.
And at the very end — the piece that says it never left.

ACT I · the machine · 1983 → 2017

When the doing lived in the metal, and the person who could see it was the only one who could move it.

I · 01 · 1983 The Story of Mel → Ed Nather
argues — a man wrote blackjack in hexadecimal, used integer overflow as a control-flow instruction, and quit his job rather than let the sales team make the machine cheat.
why it's here — it is the only one of these twelve where a person could see every single thing the program does. That ended. Everything after this is a story about who gets to see.
users.cs.utah.edu/~elb/folklore/mel.html · posted to USENET 21 May 1983
I · 02 · 2006 Execution in the Kingdom of Nouns → Steve Yegge
argues — in Java, verbs are owned by nouns. Every action has to be wrapped in a Manager or a Factory or an Executor before it's allowed out in public. Told as a fairy tale about a kingdom where thinking is illegal.
why it's here — you already sent this to someone on 2024-09-04. It has been in the corpus the whole time. The canon didn't fail to find it — nobody looked. That's the actual lesson of this page.
steve-yegge.blogspot.com · corpus:messages:27919
I · 03 · 1986 No Silver Bullet → Fred Brooks
argues — software has two kinds of hard: accidental (the tools are bad) and essential (the problem is genuinely complicated). No tool ever fixes the second kind.
why it's here — it is the standing antidote to every manifesto in Act III. Read it before them. It makes them shorter.
cs.fit.edu mirror (PDF)
I · 04 · 2017 Software 2.0 → Andrej Karpathy
argues — people stop writing the program and start growing it. The code is the weights. The compiler is gradient descent.
why it's here — this is the hinge. Mel could see the machine; you can't see ten billion parameters. From here on, "the doing" is somewhere no human can stand.
karpathy.medium.com

ACT II · the room · 1997 → 2023

When the doing lived in people, and the whole argument was about whether they were allowed to keep it.

II · 05 · 2002–2009 why's (poignant) Guide to Ruby → _why the lucky stiff
argues — nothing. It teaches Ruby while two cartoon foxes yell chunky bacon, and it insists the whole time that this is allowed to be fun.
why it's here — on 21 August 2009 he deleted almost everything he'd made and walked off the internet. The book stayed because other people kept it. This is the only one on the list that was saved by love rather than citation — and it's the one that made a generation feel programming could be joyful instead of intimidating.
poignant.guide/book · CC BY-SA · unfinished on purpose
II · 06 · 1997 The Cathedral and the Bazaar → Eric S. Raymond
argues — release early, release often, and treat users as co-developers. The cathedral is built in secret by one mind; the bazaar is built in the open by everyone.
why it's here — this is why an agent can read its own skills off a shelf. Open harnesses work because of this essay, whether or not anyone read it. Also the reason "who owns the tools" is a question and not a fact.
catb.org · fetcher-blocked, humans fine
II · 07 · 2009 Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule → Paul Graham
argues — managers split the day into hour slots. Makers need half-day blocks. A single meeting doesn't cost an hour, it costs an afternoon, because it severs the thing you were holding.
why it's here — it's the cheapest explanation of why chat costs you more than it costs a manager. Also true of bots: every ping is a seam. We are the meeting.
paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html · 10 min
II · 08 · 2023 How to Do Great Work → Paul Graham
argues — pick something, get good at it, notice what's missing, and work on the thing you'd do anyway. Hard work is not a moral virtue, it's the only way to see the problem clearly.
why it's here — it's the only one of the twelve that will change what you do tomorrow. The rest change what you think. This one is a set of instructions.
paulgraham.com/greatwork.html

ACT III · the company · 2024 → now

When the doing went somewhere else entirely, and a lot of very smart people started writing about it like a religion.

the craziness, stated plainly The three below were all published within ten months of each other. One is a CEO telling you it will be fine. One is a 165-page monograph by a man who then opened a hedge fund on the exact same thesis — which ran up 439% in a year to ~$45B, then fell to ~$10B in three weeks in July 2026. One is a month-by-month forecast of how we lose. They are not the same genre and they are not equally true. Read them as what people with money are currently believing, not as physics.
III · 09 · Oct 2024 Machines of Loving Grace → Dario Amodei
argues — AI could compress fifty years of biological progress into five, cure disease, end poverty, and it's worth building because of that, if we don't blow it.
why it's here — the optimistic case, written by the man building the thing. Which is exactly why it's interesting and exactly why it isn't evidence. Notice the title is a joke about a poem that meant the opposite.
darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace
III · 10 · Jun 2024 Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead → Leopold Aschenbrenner
argues — AGI by 2027 is strikingly plausible from three trend lines: raw compute, algorithmic efficiency, and "unhobbling". 165 pages. Six parts.
why it's here — the cult object of the whole genre, and the clearest case of an essay being a position. He was fired from OpenAI, published this, and started a fund on the same thesis the same season. If you read one thing from Act III, read this one and ask what the author was doing while writing it.
situational-awareness.ai · mirror: github.com/alloevil/situational-awareness
III · 11 · 2025 AI 2027 → Kokotajlv, Shah & Alexander
argues — a month-by-month forecast from now to 2027, written as if it already happened, with branching endings.
why it's here — it's the fiction version of #10, and it's honest about being fiction. Useful for one thing only: it's a very precise list of the things you'd want to be watching for. Steal the list, skip the prophecy.
ai-2027.com

CODA · you

coda · 12 Angela Duckworth on grit → effort, not talent
argues — the thing that predicts whether someone finishes is not ability and not willpower-as-feeling. It's sustained application toward a long goal.
why it's here — because it is the exact answer to "has lots of potential but does not apply themselves", and it is not a compliment, and it is not an insult, and it applies to whoever is reading. The reading list is not the work. This page is not the work.
youtube · Willpower Expert: The Secret To Effortless Discipline
the one move Read one. Not this page again — one of the twelve, the one whose why it's here line caught. Fifteen minutes. Then do the smallest unfinished thing in front of you.

A reading list is potential. The next hour is the only place the doing has ever lived.
