The five moves, if you read nothing else

  1. Skip CS50L and CS50x for now. Take Andrew Ng's AI for Everyone (~6 h, free to audit) — vocabulary in one weekend. [thread p13–14 recommends exactly this]
  2. Then the rule-map, not more courses: Singapore AI Verify + Model AI Governance Framework, Korea AI Basic Act (in force 2026), China GenAI rules, PDPA — one cheat-sheet each, weekly scan.
  3. Turn Slack into your case-management OS: zero-DM intake forms, threads-only detail, emoji state ledger (👀/✅/❌), keyword alerts on "indemnity / breach / PDPA".
  4. If you run an agent harness, isolate it: sandboxed runner, least-privilege tokens, egress allowlist, and a human [Confirm/Abort] gate on anything that touches the outside world.
  5. Measure one number: deflection rate. What % of routine queries got answered without a human? That's the only proof this stack is worth anything. [thread p52: target 25–30%]

B · The rule-map (what actually binds you)

thread (p14–24, p28–30): APAC is diverging — voluntary/principles-based in SG, JP, AU vs prescriptive in CN, KR. Singapore IMDA published a Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (4 dimensions: bound the autonomy, human accountability, technical controls, end-user responsibility). China = security reviews + algorithm registry; Korea = high-impact assessments. PDPA breach notification: 3 calendar days vs Australia's different timeline.
The frame that pays: you are not writing policy docs anymore, you are co-designing compliance architecture with engineers — runtime guardrails instead of post-facto audits.
Build one living table: jurisdiction × trigger × deadline × owner. Every new rule updates the row; nothing else counts as "staying current".
Rule-map, verified 2026 (primary sources fetched 2026-10-06; full runbook in AXIS/work/ACTIVE/apac-ai-law/RUNBOOK.md):
· Korea AI Basic Act — in force 22 Jan 2026: high-impact AI = 10 named sectors (self-assessment), large-scale duty at 10²⁶ FLOPs, advance AI-use notice + output labeling, foreign-firm domestic-rep thresholds, fines to KRW 30M; MSIT grace period ≈1 year → ~Jan 2027 is your real clock.
· Australia ADM transparency (APP 1.7–1.9) commences 10 Dec 2026 — hard external deadline.
· China: still no enacted AI Law (academic drafts only); binding layer = GenAI Interim Measures (extraterritorial), Deep Synthesis, Algorithm Recommendation, AI content labeling (GB 45438-2025), plus Anthropomorphic AI Interaction Measures in force 15 Jul 2026.
· Singapore: MGF for Agentic AI v1.5 (May 2026) + AI Verify toolkit; PDPC AI advisory guidelines (1 Mar 2024); PDPA data portability legislated but not yet in force.
· ASEAN: voluntary 3-document stack (AI Guide 2024 → Expanded GenAI Guide 2025 → Responsible AI Roadmap 2025–2030). No ASEAN agentic guide.
· IAPP AIGP: real but pricey ($649–799 exam + membership; 40–60 h) — employer-funded, and do it last, once the role formalises.

C · 4-Hour Workweek ↔ Muse/OpenMuse

thread (p24–27, 34–37): Ferriss's 2007 "Muse" (DEAL: Definition→Elimination→Automation→Liberation) maps 1:1 onto agent architecture: Definition = system prompts; Elimination = context/RAG hygiene; Automation = tool calling with checkpoints; Liberation = …conditional. OpenMuse = open-source self-hostable personal agent (browser + terminal + files, AG-UI swappable models).
The mapping is right and the punchline is the honest one (p36): when automation is free, the market raises the baseline. You don't get 36 hours back; you get judged on taste, contextual judgment, trust. Same as Ferriss's own footnote in practice — the point was leverage over your time, not leisure.
Apply DEAL to one recurring task this month: define its MED, eliminate its inputs, automate its 80%, and personally handle only the 20% that needs a human name on it.

F · What the thread never says (our additions)

Don't pay for a credential before you've done 2 weeks of free work with the tools. AIGP is a resume signal; AI Verify documentation is your actual job.
Your real bottleneck isn't knowledge, it's triage. Measure your own inbox for one week before automating anything — most "automation" projects fail because they automate a queue nobody measured.
Write the SOP first, agent second. If you can't hand it to a smart intern, you can't hand it to a harness.
Keep a "dated claims" file: every rule with an effective date. Regulations expire in your head faster than in the law.

R · The reading list (classics — refined)

Ten pieces, free, in order. ~6 hours total.
1 · Start here (if code is not your job)
· Paul Ford, What Is Code? (Bloomberg, 2015) — 38k words written for the educated civilian; skim, don't study. ~2h
· Paul Graham, Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule (2009) — 10 min; the real reason Slack meetings cost you more than they cost engineers. 10 min
· Joel Spolsky, The Law of Leaky Abstractions (2002) — every tool hides complexity and then leaks it; your jargon-drowning problem, explained. 15 min
2 · How software actually gets made
· Eric S. Raymond, The Cathedral and the Bazaar (1997) — why open harnesses, shared skills and "release early, release often" produce the tools you're about to use. (canonical link; our fetcher is blocked from catb.org — human browsers work) ~1h
· Fred Brooks, No Silver Bullet (1986) — the AI-hype antidote: essential vs accidental complexity; why "AI writes the code" fixes the cheap part and not the hard part. 30 min
· Andrej Karpathy, Software 2.0 (2017) — models as a new programming medium; why "prompt, evaluate, curate" is now a real job. (Medium blocks bots; humans fine) 20 min
3 · Work & taste (the PG pack)
· PG, How to Do Great Work (2023) — the long map: choosing a field, working on what you like, taste. ~1.5h
· PG, Do Things That Don't Scale (2013) — the pilot-first rule for every Slack/harness automation: do it manually for one team before you build. 15 min
· PG, The Hundred-Year Language (2003) — why tool choices compound; skim for the argument, not the language. 20 min
· PG index — everything else lives here; Beating the Averages is the next one to read after these.
