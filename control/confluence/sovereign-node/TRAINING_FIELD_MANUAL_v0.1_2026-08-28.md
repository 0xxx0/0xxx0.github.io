# TRAINING FIELD MANUAL v0.1

**Date:** 2026-08-28  
**Status:** generated reconstruction; evidence-audited; first execution version  
**Objective:** recover the strongest practical TRAINING lineage, identify what actually survived, and turn it into one small system for rapid skill acquisition, safe object calibration, memory, calculation, making, feedback, retention, and tested transfer.

## Assumptions

- The present state is overloaded and depleted enough that restoration precedes difficult practice.
- “Bene Gesserit” is a fictional interface and atmosphere, not an authority.
- “Throwing” means gentle practice with rolled socks, lightweight beanbags, or foam balls in a cleared, pet-free area.
- Broad transfer is never assumed. Every claimed transfer must survive a held-out test.
- The system should produce useful artifacts and observable capability, not a large curriculum that exists only as prose.

---

## 0. Compression

The project already contains the organs of a serious training system:

> state control → perception → prediction → action → measured error → correction → delayed retrieval → tested transfer → reusable artifact

What was missing was a spine. The spine is:

> **Predict → attempt → observe → classify → change one variable → retest without aid → promote only what survives.**

This loop works across soft-object tossing, rhythm, knots, calculation, memory, making, explanation, and the use of external tools.

### The four governing laws

1. **Performance during practice is not learning.** Learning is retained change measured later.
2. **Near transfer is normal; far transfer is exceptional.** Design bridge tasks instead of assuming general enhancement.
3. **A measurement must name a real variable.** Timing error, radial error, recall, and task completion are valid; “mental harmony” is not yet a signal.
4. **The ratchet must remain after the session.** A log, fixture, card, benchmark, recording, or tested procedure survives every excursion.

## 4. Evidence audit: what survives and what breaks

| Claim or method | Current verdict | Operational consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Task-specific practice | Strong, unsurprising core | Practice the actual perceptual and motor decisions, not only generic exercises. |
| Delayed testing | Essential distinction | Judge learning from next-day or one-week no-aid tests, not the last fluent block. |
| External focus always wins | Not supported as a universal law | A/B test an outcome cue against a body cue; retain the cue that improves delayed performance. |
| Random practice always beats blocked practice | Too broad | Calibrate with blocked trials; introduce serial or random variation only after the mapping is manageable. |
| Contextual interference | Medium laboratory transfer effect; weaker applied evidence | Treat mixed practice as a transfer probe, not a compulsory opening condition. |
| Differential learning / maximal variability | Promising but overmarketed | Vary task-relevant dimensions one at a time; do not add chaos for its own sake. |
| Reduced feedback is inherently superior | No robust average advantage | Give interpretable outcome feedback, but include mandatory no-feedback probes. |
| Short rests produce micro-consolidation | 2025–2026 evidence challenges this interpretation | Use rests to recover attention and planning; do not count post-break freshness as learning. |
| Sleep | Practical priority; motor-memory benefit is modest and task-dependent | Do not trade ordinary sleep for extra repetitions; use next-day tests. |
| Action observation + imagery | Useful adjunct; evidence heterogeneous | Watch a few clear repetitions, imagine briefly, then execute. Do not replace practice. |
| Working-memory “brain training” | Near transfer; broad far transfer mostly null | Train the real calculation, memory, or decision task. |
| Method of loci | Strong immediate recall signal, but evidence quality is low–very low | Use it for ordered material, compare it against retrieval practice, and test after delay. |
| Mental abacus | Exceptional trained arithmetic is possible; broad transfer uncertain | Optional long-term specialization, not a general cognitive shortcut. |
| Slow comfortable breathing | Immediate HRV effects; emotional effects less certain | Use as a brief state gate, not as a plasticity claim or endurance contest. |
| Movement sonification | Promising across many systems; retention evidence remains sparse | Map a verified movement variable to sound, remove the sound for final trials, and test retention. |
| EEG neurofeedback | Promising laboratory signal with bias and heterogeneity | Not first-line; direct outcomes and fixed-camera video come first. |
| Consumer wearable scores | Useful for standardized trends, not ground truth | Use the same device/context; never let proprietary readiness scores overrule direct performance. |

### Cutting-edge signals worth preserving

1. **Prediction plus feedback may calibrate tool use quickly.** A 2026 two-experiment study found that five prediction–feedback trials improved metacognitive calibration and later reminder decisions; prediction without feedback did not. This is the strongest universal addition to the kernel.
2. **Sonification now has a usable taxonomy.** A 2026 systematic review separates parameter-mapped, musically informed, single-tone, rhythmic, music-based, and environmental sound; triggers may be event-, error-, trajectory-, or continuous.
3. **The famous external-focus advantage may be mostly reporting bias.** Corrected average effects in a 2024 robust Bayesian reanalysis were near zero.
4. **Micro-offline “learning during ten-second rests” is disputed.** New work suggests transient recovery and preplanning can mimic consolidation.
5. **Errorless learning is not generally superior.** A 2026 meta-analysis found no consistent overall movement advantage and low-certainty subgroup effects.

## 6. Empirical “Bene Gesserit × Mentat” replacement

This is a transformative, evidence-grounded field school. It does not reproduce the fan manuals.

### Page 0 — Instrument panel

Track five faculties:

- **A — accuracy**
- **R — delayed retention**
- **L — latency**, considered after accuracy
- **C — calibration**, predicted versus actual performance
- **T — held-out transfer**

Run three baselines on different days before interpreting change:

1. Recall 20 neutral words immediately and after 24 hours.
2. Solve 20 mixed arithmetic items and assign each answer 50%, 70%, 90%, or 99% confidence.
3. Inspect 12 ordinary objects for 45 seconds; report attributes and locations from memory.
4. Explain one concept for 60 seconds; compare the recording with an automatic transcript.

### Page 1 — State gate

For three to five minutes, breathe comfortably and slightly slower than usual while attending to one neutral anchor. No holds, rapid breathing, or challenge framing.

When attention wanders, label only the category—sound, thought, sensation—then return.

**Metric:** unplanned departures from the task.  
**Progression:** three minutes → five → eight; add ordinary background noise only after the base condition stabilizes.

### Page 2 — Observation as controlled sampling

Arrange 8–12 ordinary objects. Inspect for 45 seconds using a fixed schema:

1. count;
2. identity;
3. color/material;
4. relative position;
5. anomaly.

Cover the scene, write the report, then verify against a photo.

**Metrics:** correct details, false details, missed objects, correct spatial relations.  
**Boundary:** train observation of neutral scenes and task material, not supposed motive-reading or lie detection.

### Page 3 — Retrieval × spacing × feedback

Create ten question–answer prompts from material that matters.

1. Attempt recall before looking.
2. Reveal and correct immediately.
3. Continue until each item can be produced correctly.
4. Relearn on days 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30.

**Metric:** unprompted recall on days 7 and 30.  
**Progression:** facts → explanations → applications → mixed cues.

### Page 4 — Loci and structured imagery

Choose a stable ten-stop route through a familiar place. Attach one item to each stop through a distinctive interaction. Retrieve forward, backward, and from randomly named stops.

**Metrics:** item accuracy, order accuracy, and 24-hour recall.  
**Control condition:** compare loci with ordinary retrieval practice; retain loci only where it wins.

### Page 5 — Chunk construction

A chunk exposes structure rather than merely shortening notation.

For each skill, encode:

- primitive;
- relation;
- trigger;
- operation;
- exception;
- verification example.

**Test:** reconstruct the chunk from its trigger and apply it to a novel example.

### Page 6 — Prospective memory and calibrated offloading

For three ordinary tasks, create both:

- an if–then cue: “When X occurs, perform Y”; and
- an external reminder at the actual decision point.

Before choosing internal memory or a reminder, predict unaided success. Afterward, compare the forecast with actual performance.

**Metric:** intended-time completion over seven days plus forecast error.  
**Law:** external memory is part of competence; internalize only what must remain available without the tool.

### Page 7 — Arithmetic engine

Sequence:

1. place value and magnitude;
2. decomposition and compensation;
3. arithmetic-fact retrieval;
4. estimation;
5. exact computation;
6. independent verification.

Examples:

- `68 + 47 = (68 + 40) + 7 = 115`
- `23 × 16 = 23 × (10 + 6) = 368`
- `15% of 260 = 10% + 5% = 39`

**Test:** 20 mixed problems; record strategy and error type.  
**Gate:** add time pressure only after at least 95% accuracy in two sessions.  
**Verification:** estimate first, then use an inverse operation. Casting out nines is a secondary checksum that misses many errors.

### Page 8 — Estimation and calibration

For five verifiable quantities, state:

- lower bound;
- best estimate;
- upper bound;
- probability that the interval contains the answer.

Verify afterward. Classify multiplicative error: within `1.25×`, `2×`, `4×`, or worse.

For binary judgments, record probability `p` before checking:

\[
\text{Brier score}=\operatorname{mean}(p-y)^2
\]

where `y = 1` when correct and `0` otherwise. Lower is better.

### Page 9 — Regulation under ordinary load

Use an ordinary, mildly demanding task.

1. Name the current state without explanation.
2. Take five minutes of comfortable slow breathing.
3. Choose either reappraisal—change the task’s meaning—or acceptance—allow the state while continuing.
4. Repeat with an equivalent item set.

**Metrics:** accuracy, latency, and recovery; subjective intensity alone is insufficient.

### Page 10 — Voice and posture as transmission

Use a comfortable upright position, a free jaw, sufficient breath, and ordinary volume. Record a 60-second instruction.

Score:

- transcription errors;
- filler count;
- unfinished sentences;
- listener or reviewer summary accuracy;
- whether the requested action was unambiguous.

Voice serves clarity and consent, never domination.

### Page 11 — Integration and transfer audit

Weekly 15-minute circuit:

1. inspect a neutral scene;
2. encode five facts;
3. solve five arithmetic items;
4. estimate one unknown quantity;
5. recall the facts;
6. explain the result;
7. assign confidence.

Classify every failure as perception, encoding, retrieval, computation, verification, calibration, or communication. Train the failed component directly, then retest with new material.

### Hard myth cuts

- No photographic page imprinting.
- No inherited ancestral memory.
- No left-brain/right-brain personality split.
- No eye-direction or microexpression lie detector.
- No coercive “Voice.”
- No perpetual awareness.
- No mental-math trick as proof of general intelligence.
- No assumption that memory-sport or abacus gains transfer broadly.
- No DIY sleep cueing or consumer brain stimulation.

## 7. Safe throwing as a calibration laboratory

The reason to begin with soft-object target practice is not that throwing is supreme. It exposes a complete learning loop in visible form:

> target → prediction → release → flight → impact → bias/noise diagnosis → recalibration

### Safety envelope

- Use only rolled socks, lightweight beanbags, or foam balls.
- Throw toward a soft target/backstop, never toward people, animals, glass, electronics, doors, roads, or balconies.
- Stop ceiling fans; clear the full path; keep cats outside the area.
- Use gentle underhand arcs indoors.
- No maximal-force throws, hard/weighted objects, blind catches, dives, or fast tethered-prop work.
- Stop for pain, dizziness, loss of coordination, or repeated chasing of misses.

### Tomorrow’s first baseline — 8 to 10 minutes

1. Put a laundry basket or five-zone pillow target about 1.5 m away.
2. Make 10 gentle underhand tosses with each hand.
3. Record hits and predicted impact zone before each attempt.
4. Practise for three minutes with one cue only.
5. Repeat 10 tosses per hand.
6. Before any practice the following day, repeat the 10-per-hand cold test.

The next-day score matters more than the immediate improvement.

### Measurement levels

**Level 1 — minimal**

- hits out of 10;
- predicted zone versus actual zone;
- first attempt after a target change;
- next-day cold score.

**Level 2 — grid**

For each impact record horizontal and vertical error `(xᵢ, yᵢ)` from the target center.

\[
RE_i=\sqrt{x_i^2+y_i^2}
\]

\[
MRE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i RE_i
\]

Mean radial error measures overall accuracy. The average `x` and `y` locate systematic bias; spread around that center estimates execution noise.

| Pattern | Diagnosis | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Repeatable off-center cluster | calibration bias | Adjust aim/cue while preserving the repeatable motion. |
| Centered but widely dispersed | execution noise | Shorten distance or enlarge target; stabilize tempo. |
| Off-center and dispersed | task too difficult, ambiguous, or loaded | Simplify; restore one known condition. |
| Centered and tight | stable base | Change exactly one variable. |

### Twelve-session progression

| Session | Main condition | Held-out question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fixed object, distance, target; both hands | What are baseline bias and spread? |
| 2 | Large target, same gentle underhand arc | Does repeatability improve? |
| 3 | Inspect cluster every five attempts | Does bias fall without spread increasing? |
| 4 | Near/middle/far in blocked order | Can each distance be calibrated? |
| 5 | Near/middle/far in serial order | What is first-attempt error after a switch? |
| 6 | Gently mixed distances | Does recalibration survive variation? |
| 7 | Predict the impact quadrant before every attempt | Does confidence become calibrated? |
| 8 | Alternate hands | Does either hand improve or interfere? |
| 9 | Release on a slow metronome beat | Does timing reduce or increase spread? |
| 10 | Same drill, metronome removed for final trials | Was learning cue-dependent? |
| 11 | One novel soft object or target size | What is the transfer cost? |
| 12 | No-warm-up original and novel tests | What survives? |

### Practice rule

Start blocked when the mapping is new. Move to serial variation, then gentle randomization only after the base action is manageable. Random practice is an experiment, not a virtue.

## 8. The A3 training cassette

Use one reusable gatefold chassis: two A3 sheets of corrugated cardboard or polypropylene closing to roughly A4. Binder clips, tape, index cards, and elastic loops hold modules. Visual grammar: black border, white field, orange for active/error, blue for alternate/recovery.

### Module set

| Module | Minimum form | Trained variables | Acceptance test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Soft-Toss Gate** | Five-zone fabric target, fixed floor line, three rolled socks, phone off-axis | calibration, bias, spread, prediction, hand switching | 30 attempts remain contained; scoring is repeatable; target does not shift materially. |
| **State Palace** | Six-panel accordion, 12 cue/answer cards, one state token | retrieval, order, confidence, procedure memory | A six-step procedure can be reconstructed after a 20-minute delay. |
| **Route Loom** | 3×4 punched-hole board, two shoelaces, eight constraint cards | planning, bimanual sequencing, topology, repair | Three puzzles self-check without ambiguous rules; reset under 30 seconds. |
| **Pulse Rail** | Two contrasting tap pads plus phone recorder/metronome | timing variance, left/right lag, recovery after change | 100 taps are separable; one five-minute sequence produces a scoreable record. |
| **Coordinate Fan** | Eight-sector/three-ring radial face, 6×4 grid reverse, command slips | mental rotation, switching, sequence memory | Deploy under 10 seconds; 20 commands score unambiguously. |

### First build: State Palace + Soft-Toss Gate

Time budget: 45–75 minutes.

Materials:

- spare pillowcase or fabric target;
- removable tape or binder clips;
- black, orange, and blue markers;
- three identical rolled socks;
- A4/A3 cardstock;
- 12 index cards;
- phone and stable stand.

Build only the five-zone target and six-panel card deck first. Do not add sensors until the manual version has produced three usable sessions.

### NeuronBenchWork / sonification minimal organism

Begin with timing, because it is directly observable.

1. Record 100 alternating taps against a metronome.
2. Estimate inter-onset intervals from audio or button timestamps.
3. Define error as `actual interval − target interval`.
4. Map absolute error to audible roughness or pitch distance; map the sign of error to left/right or low/high.
5. Use sound for a short feedback block.
6. Finish with silent trials.
7. Retest silently the next day.

The “movement toward harmony” becomes rigorous only when consonance tracks a defined reduction in timing or trajectory error. It must never stand in for an inferred mental state.

Later hardware may use low-voltage ESP32 buttons with timestamp logging. No mains work is required.

## 9. The 72-hour restart

### Day 0 — recover the floor

- Complete the immediate return sequence.
- Prepare the soft-object baseline.
- Do no demanding session.
- Put all expansion ideas in `PARKED`.

### Day 1 — establish truth

- Run the 8–10 minute soft-toss baseline.
- Run five arithmetic items and five memory prompts; predict confidence before checking.
- Build the six-panel State Palace or draw its layout.
- Record one seven-field ledger row.

### Day 2 — retest, then perturb

- Run the cold toss retest before practice.
- Retest yesterday’s five memory prompts.
- Change one toss variable only: cue, hand order, or distance.
- Clear one additional home/work cell for 15 minutes.
- Decide whether the first module passes its acceptance test.

### Day 3 — promote the organism

- Repeat the original toss condition and the one-variable condition.
- Run one 15-minute integrated mental circuit.
- Compare predicted and actual outcomes.
- Write one line each under `KEEP / CHANGE / PARK`.
- Prepare Week 1 without adding a second primary motor discipline.

### Readiness menus

| State | Session |
|---|---|
| **Low (1–2/5)** | Two minutes easy movement; four to six minutes gentle rehearsal, cards, or observation; stop. |
| **Medium (3/5)** | Five-minute warm-up; two six-minute blocks; two-minute ledger. |
| **High (4–5/5)** | Five-to-eight-minute warm-up; three eight-to-ten-minute blocks; maximum 40 minutes total. |

### Stop rules

End the physical session when:

- pain appears or increases;
- dizziness, unusual breathing difficulty, sudden headache, or loss of coordination occurs;
- accuracy drops more than roughly 20% from the first set;
- two sets deteriorate consecutively;
- movement becomes forceful, rushed, or uncontrolled;
- the single variable under test can no longer be named.

Do not make up missed training with a larger session.

## 10. Six-week progression

Use soft-object calibration as the primary motor experiment. Mental pages and the build cassette support it; they do not become competing full curricula.

| Week | Primary question | Motor experiment | Mental experiment | Artifact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0 — Baseline** | What is true now? | Three baseline sessions | Three memory/arithmetic/calibration baselines | Seven-field ledger |
| **1 — Floor** | Can the loop start and stop cleanly? | Fixed target, object, distance, cue | State gate + retrieval | Soft-Toss Gate |
| **2 — Retention** | What survives a delay? | Distributed short sessions versus one equal-total block | Days 1/3/7 retrieval | State Palace |
| **3 — Cueing** | Which instruction helps this task? | Outcome cue versus body cue, matched attempts | Loci versus ordinary retrieval | Cue cards |
| **4 — Variability** | Which variation improves recalibration? | Blocked versus serial/mixed distance | Mixed arithmetic strategies | Coordinate Fan or Route Loom |
| **5 — Transfer** | What crosses a boundary? | Novel target/object/hand order | New material and estimation | Transfer graph |
| **6 — Consolidation** | What deserves promotion? | Original baseline + novel test | Integrated held-out circuit | One-page playbook + specimen |

### Weekly rhythm

- **Monday:** acquisition and baseline-quality attempts.
- **Tuesday:** low-load recall and home reset.
- **Wednesday:** acquisition plus one perturbation.
- **Thursday:** rest, observation, or gentle mental rehearsal.
- **Friday:** variability and silent/no-aid test.
- **Saturday:** transfer into one practical making task.
- **Sunday:** baseline review, cat/home maintenance, prepare the next week.

### Minimum viable daily loop

> **Restore → Protect → Practise → Ratchet**

- Restore: water, ordinary food, washing, daylight or easy walking.
- Protect: cats plus one small home hazard or clutter cell.
- Practise: one skill, one variable, 6–30 minutes.
- Ratchet: record the result and leave the next setup ready.

A day counts when Restore + Protect + six minutes of practice occur.

## 11. Seven-field ledger

Do not build a dashboard until this survives two weeks.

```text
DATE / TASK / VERSION:
1. READINESS (sleep/energy/coordination 1–5):
2. ATTEMPTS + MINUTES:
3. DIRECT RESULT (accuracy or error):
4. VARIABILITY / RECURRING FAILURE:
5. PREDICTION + CONFIDENCE → ACTUAL:
6. NEXT-DAY COLD RETEST:
7. HELD-OUT TRANSFER RESULT:

NEXT SINGLE VARIABLE:
KEEP / CHANGE / PARK:
```

### Progression rules

- Progress after two stable sessions with controlled effort and no adverse next-day load.
- Change one variable: attempts, distance, target size, tempo, complexity, or session length.
- If accuracy drops more than 20%, restore the previous condition.
- If direct results improve but no-aid retention does not, treat the aid as performance support, not learning.
- Keep an experiment for three comparable sessions unless it is uncomfortable, unsafe, or obviously deteriorating performance.

### N-of-1 transfer protocol

1. Baseline source, bridge, and target on three occasions.
2. Train only the source task for several matched short sessions.
3. Retest the target without the training aid after 24–48 hours.
4. Repeat with a new held-out target variant.
5. Promote only if improvement exceeds baseline noise and survives replication.

This is not formal population science; it is sufficient to prevent storytelling from silently becoming architecture.

## 16. Deferred until the organism survives

- Complete dashboard or ontology.
- EEG/EMG/neurofeedback purchases or builds.
- Unvalidated readiness or “brain state” scoring.
- High-risk balance or speed practice while depleted.
- Hard or weighted projectiles and uncontrolled tethered props.
- Extreme breath/cold/stress stacking.
- Multiple simultaneous primary motor disciplines.
- New equipment purchases unless one missing item blocks the current safe loop.

### Promotion gate

A method becomes canonical only when it has:

1. a stable setup;
2. a named variable;
3. a baseline;
4. at least three comparable trials or sessions;
5. a delayed no-aid test;
6. a safety/stop rule;
7. a surviving artifact.

## Final compression

Do not train “generality.” Train a real task, expose its error, predict before feedback, test after delay, and build explicit bridges to the next task.
