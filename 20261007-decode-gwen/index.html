02 · the rooms

Every word below lives in a room. The same word can be furniture in one and a fuse in another. Four rooms, for a start:
the desk
Contracts, versions, sign-offs, the deal. Here a word is a promise.
so what → On the desk, ask what a change would do — who could rely on it, and when.
the machine room
Models, prompts, passes, numbers. Here a word is a setting.
so what → Instructions, not guarantees. Ask what it read, and what a number was measured on.
the cellar
Taste, patience, difference. Here a word is a description — and the thing doesn't read it.
so what → Describe before you name. The wine never argues; it just shows you.
the day
Everything else. Here a word is a mood.
so what → Most traps live here, and nobody taught us this deck.
1 questionAsk, in writing, before you fight a number: "What would show this improved — which questions, measured on which set, and how?"

03 · The deck — every word, both meanings

When you see a red 2+ SENSES badge: that word is a trap. It means two different things in two different rooms, and people act like it means one. The badge counts rooms, not importance.

04 · slips from the rounds

Ten sentences doing the rounds in the four rooms — deal desks, model rooms, cellars, days. Not strawmen: each is the pattern the deck was built for, and the trap line says which rooms the sentence is standing in at once. The sentence is not the problem. The two rooms are.
“We've not been able to prompt our way to better accuracy.”
prompt2 SENSES
The sentence everyone has said once.
so what → Prompt = instructions. When instructions stop paying: split the question, fix the cut, demand the quote, freeze a little set. Structure beats vocabulary.
machine room
“The model is 95% accurate, so we only review the 5%.”
accuracy2 SENSES
A number pretending to be a policy.
so what → 95% of which questions, weighted how, on how many documents? And who reviews the 5% — by quote or by vibe? Ask for the set before you trust the split.
machine room
“Can we fine-tune it to stop missing this data point?”
fine-tuning2 SENSES
Two words, both doing double duty.
so what → One missed question is usually not a training problem. Check what it actually read first — segmentation and retrieval — before paying for a heavier artifact.
machine room
“Reviewed — looks right.”
review2 SENSES
A verdict with no name, no date, no quote.
so what → Reviewed by ⟨name⟩ on ⟨date⟩, against ⟨the clause⟩. That sentence is the product. Without it, the word is furniture.
deal desk
“The vendor says it's grounded; there's no quote in the answer.”
grounded2 SENSES
If it's grounded, the quote is the receipt.
so what → No quote → not grounded → route it. That's not distrust; that's the rule working.
deal desk · machine room
“It signed off on the change-of-control point, but we asked about assignment.”
assignment2 SENSES
Two questions wearing one sentence — a double-barrel.
so what → Machines fail on double-barrels. Split: direct assignment, and indirect transfer — one truth each, scored separately.
deal desk
“We'll taste it next month — it needs time anyway.”
time2 SENSES
Patience as a plan.
so what → Some judgments read earlier than others. 'Needs age to reveal itself' is also true of drafts and deals: schedule the re-read, don't force the verdict.
cellar
“Same thing, cheaper.”
same2 SENSES
The most dangerous sentence in taste and in procurement.
so what → If it were the same, one of them wouldn't exist. Find the difference that changes the choice — then decide.
cellar · deal desk
“Don't read the example until you've tried it once.”
example2 SENSES
Instructions make players; examples make copiers.
so what → Play first, read later. A first attempt is data; a copied attempt is only agreement.
the day
“Is it cute?”
cute2 SENSES
A complete review in one syllable.
so what → Some verdicts need one word, not a report. Know which review you're giving — and keep the right to give the short one.

When overwhelmed, three questions, in order: (1) What must be true? (2) What is the deadline? (3) Who owns it? Everything else is weather.
