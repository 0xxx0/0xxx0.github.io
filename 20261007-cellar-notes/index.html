CELLAR NOTES.
twenty-four cards
for tasting attention.

Not a course and not advice — practice. The same discipline as the domaines list: describe before naming, keep the difference that matters, leave what is unresolved unresolved. Small pours, water, and the door open.

01

Same grape, different voice

Two bottles made from the same grape in nearby vineyards can read like cousins rather than twins. Soil, weather, and the small choices in the cellar each leave a fingerprint in the glass. Difference-within-similarity is not a flaw; it is the thing worth your attention.

practice Pour two similar wines side by side and name one thing that differs — not which is better.

02

One degree of difference matters

Often the interesting difference is tiny: a shift in ripeness, a few hours of soaking, one racking less. Small differences are easy to skip because the wines still look alike in your mind. Attention is the tool that finds them.

practice Hold both glasses and ask what is the smallest difference you can actually notice. Write down that one thing.

03

Twin bottles diverge quietly

Two bottles from the same case can drift apart with time, storage, or a cork's small chemistry. The label promises sameness; the liquid delivers history. Treat each bottle as its own event.

practice If you taste the same wine twice, note what changed between the bottles instead of forcing consistency.

04

Describe first, name later

The moment you name a thing (leather, cherry), your attention tends to settle and stop looking. Description is slower and more honest than a label. A wine person is a person who pays attention, not a person who knows more names.

practice Spend the first minute saying only what you sense — texture, weight, temperature, plain impressions — before any flavor noun.

05

Notice shape before vocabulary

Before the words for smells, there is shape: rising, dense, sharp, soft, fast, slow. Shape is available to anyone, with no training and no glossary. It usually holds more truth than the fanciest noun.

practice Describe the wine's shape in three plain words before you attempt a single flavor word.

06

Naming can close attention

A name is a door, and it can also be a lid. Once you decide a wine is typical, you stop seeing the parts that do not fit. Keep one part of you willing to revise.

practice After naming something, ask what in the glass does not fit that name — and look specifically for it.

07

Time in bottle changes readability

Young wine is loud and crowded; the parts talk over each other. With time the noise settles and structure becomes legible, the way a room becomes readable when the party ends. Patience is not waiting; it is letting the wine finish its sentence.

practice Taste a young wine slowly over an hour and note what becomes clearer as it opens.

08

Wildness becomes clear when held

Rough, wild, or ungainly notes often soften into character rather than disappear. Held with patience, what first reads as disorder can turn out to be difference that had not settled yet. The reward goes to the person who does not rush to judge.

practice When something reads as wild or rough, wait before deciding. Describe how it changes over ten minutes.

09

Patience is a tasting tool

Most of what is worth noticing arrives in the second or third look, not the first sip. Patience costs nothing, and it converts reaction into perception. It is the cheapest upgrade to any tasting.

practice Give each wine two full quiet minutes before your first words about it.

10

The maker's hand, lightly visible

Winemaking is a series of interventions: when to pick, how long to soak, which vessel to trust. The best of them make the grape's differences easier to read, the way good light helps a painting. The hand should show up as clarity, not as signature.

practice Ask of a wine what was done here that helps me see the fruit — not whose style is this.

11

Reveal, carry, then release

Three jobs, in order: reveal what the grapes already hold, carry it safely through the year, and release it in a form that speaks plainly. Each job can be overdone — extraction that shouts, vessels that dominate, adjustments that smooth away character. Do enough to make difference legible, not so much that the intervention becomes the subject.

practice In any glass, try to hear the fruit and the maker's work as two separate voices.

12

Intervention should not become subject

When the work of making becomes the loudest thing in the glass, you are tasting technique, not place. That can be interesting, but it is a different study. Keep the distinction in your notes.

practice Write one line about the fruit and one line about the craft, and notice which one the glass emphasizes.

13

What arrives first, lingers

Aroma is not a single chord; it is a sequence. Some things greet you at the rim of the glass, some arrive with the sip, some stay after. The order and the length are part of the taste.

practice Smell, sip, then wait in silence for thirty seconds and note what is still there.

14

Aroma has an order

The first impression is often the most volatile and the least reliable; the quietest notes are usually the ones with staying power. What arrives first is not the most important — it is just the fastest. Train yourself to hear the whole sequence.

practice Write three aroma notes in the order they appear, not in order of impressiveness.

15

Rate of change is information

A wine that shifts as it sits is telling you about itself — youth, air, temperature, structure. A wine that never changes is telling you something too. Change over ten minutes is data, not noise.

practice Note one difference between the first smell and the last, and record the time between them.

16

The taster is the instrument

You are not a neutral meter; you are the measuring device, and you drift. Sleep, mood, the room, and who is beside you all move the reading. Knowing your own drift is what keeps your notes honest.

practice Before tasting, write one sentence about how you feel today. Later, check it against what you wrote about the wine.

17

Glass and temperature shape reading

The glass is a lens: its shape gathers or scatters aroma. Temperature decides what speaks — cold mutes and sharpens, warmth opens and softens. Neither one changes the wine; both change the reading.

practice Taste the same wine at two temperatures or in two glasses, and record which notes appear in only one condition.

18

Hunger and company alter perception

An empty stomach and a full one produce different wines, and so do a quiet table and a loud one. Attention needs a baseline of care to do its work. Choose conditions that let you listen.

practice Eat something plain first, and taste at least one wine in silence before joining the conversation.

19

Log the one difference

A tasting note does not need a score to be useful. What survives is the one difference that mattered — the thing you would tell a friend. That single honest line is worth more than a column of numbers.

practice After each wine, write the one difference that mattered, in plain words.

20

Four lines beat a score

Keep a simple log: date, place, first impression, and the one difference that mattered. No numbers are required; numbers often hide what they pretend to summarize. A small honest record grows into real memory.

practice Use the same four lines every time so your notes can be compared across months.

21

Write what surprised you

Surprise is a signal that your attention met something real. The expected parts of a wine fade from memory; the unexpected ones stay if you catch them. Write the surprise while it is fresh.

practice Keep one running line per bottle that begins "I did not expect..." and finish it honestly.

22

Small pours keep senses sharp

A large pour asks the palate to work hard and lets the nose fog up. Small pours keep the reading clean and leave the wine room to change. Attention lasts longer in small sips.

practice Pour less than feels natural, and leave room in the glass for air and for a second look.

23

Water resets the palate

Water between wines clears the stage: it washes residue and gives the senses a beat of quiet. Plain water is the only reset a tasting needs. Everything else adds noise to the reading.

practice Sip water and take one slow breath before moving on to the next wine.

24

Notice when fatigue arrives

Palate fatigue is real, and it arrives quietly: flavors flatten, differences blur, effort rises. It is not weakness; it is a signal to pause. Respect it and the day stays honest.

practice When two wines start tasting the same, stop and note that the instrument is tired — not the wines.
